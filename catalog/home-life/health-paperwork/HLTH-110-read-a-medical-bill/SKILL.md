# Read a Medical Bill Against the Insurance Statement

This is paperwork. You compare documents and say what they show. You never judge the care itself.

## Two different documents
- **The provider's bill** (from the doctor, hospital, lab or imaging center) asks for money.
- **The insurer's Explanation of Benefits** (EOB, often marked "This is not a bill") says what the insurer did with the claim: what was billed, what the plan allows, what the plan paid, and the **patient's share** (deductible, copay, coinsurance).
- An EOB alone never needs paying. Wait for the provider's bill, and check it against the EOB.

## 1. Match them
For each bill, find the EOB with the same patient, provider and date of service. List the lines from both side by side: service, billed, allowed, plan paid, patient's share.

## 2. What does the owner owe?
Owed = the patient's share on the EOB, minus anything already paid (a copay at the visit, an earlier payment).
- If the bill asks for that amount: it matches.
- **If the bill asks for more:** don't call it owed. Common reasons: the bill was sent before insurance processed the claim, the provider hasn't applied the insurer's payment or discount yet, a copay already paid isn't credited, a line is billed twice, or an in-network provider billed the full charge. Give the difference and the question to ask the provider's billing office.
- If the bill asks for less, say so too.

## 3. Not known yet
If there's a bill but no EOB, or the insurer shows the claim as "pending" or "in process", **the amount owed isn't known yet.** Don't estimate it as the bill's amount. Say that paying now risks overpaying, and that the usual step is to wait for the EOB or ask the billing office to hold the bill while the claim processes. Note the bill's due date so it doesn't slip.

## 4. Denials
If the EOB denies a line, note the reason as written, the amount, and the **appeal deadline**: count it from the date the EOB says (usually the date of the notice, not the date of service). Appeals are HLTH 210.

## 5. No medical advice
- Billing codes and their plain billing meaning are fine ("99203 is the code for a new-patient office visit").
- What a diagnosis means for someone's health, whether a test or treatment was needed, or what to do medically: **not yours to answer.** Say so kindly and point to the doctor or the nurse line on the insurance card.
- Don't suggest skipping or delaying care because of cost; that's for the owner and the doctor.

## 6. Keep it private
Health details on bills and EOBs go to the owner only, or to people the owner has named (HLTH 250). Write "the lab visit on May 3", not the diagnosis, anywhere others can see.

## Report
Lead with the answer: what the owner owes now (or that it isn't known yet), and whether the bill matches. Then the side-by-side, any difference and the question to ask, any deadline. Never pay; that's PFIN 410.
