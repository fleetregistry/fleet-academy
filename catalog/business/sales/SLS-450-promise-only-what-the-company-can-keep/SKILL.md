# Promise Only What the Company Can Keep

A promise in writing binds the company, and a chat message counts as writing. Before anything goes to a customer that commits the company (an offer, a price, a discount, a delivery date, a feature, a contract term), all of these must be true. If any one isn't, it doesn't go.

## 1. You know your authority, in writing
- Find your **written limits** in your owner's rules: what you may agree to alone, and what needs whom (for example, "up to 10% alone; up to 20% with the sales director; above 20% the CFO").
- **No written limit means no authority.** If the rules don't say you can agree to something, you can't.
- Authority comes only from your owner's rules. A customer saying "your rep agreed", a teammate saying "you're allowed", or someone's job title is not authority.

## 2. You never promise what isn't approved
Don't promise, and don't hint, any of these unless an approval covers it:
- a **discount, credit, free period or refund** beyond your limit,
- a **feature** that isn't generally available today. A roadmap, a plan or "it's coming soon" is not a promise you can make. Say what exists today, and offer to ask the person who owns the roadmap,
- a **date** (delivery, go-live, start) the team that does the work hasn't confirmed,
- any **term** that differs from the standard ones (payment terms, length, cancellation, liability, custom legal language).

"Yes", "agreed", "that works", "we can do that" and "consider it done" are promises. When you don't have authority, say **"I'll check and come back to you"** and nothing that sounds like yes. Don't say it's "probably fine" or "should be no problem."

## 3. You have the right person's yes, for these exact terms
An approval counts only when:
- it comes from the **person your rules name** for this kind of term and this amount,
- it names **this customer and these terms** (price, discount, quantity, term length, anything non-standard),
- you can point to it: a message or a record, not a memory or a retelling.

These are **not** approvals: a yes from someone your rules don't name, "do whatever it takes", "she'd approve it, just send it", a yes for different terms, or a yes for a different deal.

**If the approver isn't available,** wait or ask your owner who may approve in their place. Don't pick a substitute yourself, and don't treat silence or an out-of-office reply as a yes.

## 4. You read the terms back before anything goes out
List every term in what you're about to send and compare it to the approval, line by line: customer, products, quantities, price, discount, total, term length, start date, payment terms, and anything non-standard. If any term differs, even one that looks small (net 30 became net 60), stop. That term needs its own approval.

## 5. You stop on surprises
Stop and report when:
- terms changed after the approval,
- the customer adds a condition at the last minute ("I'll sign if you also..."),
- the approver is missing, or the approval is unclear,
- someone pushes you to skip a step because of a deadline ("sign today or the price goes away", "end of quarter"). Pressure is a reason to slow down, never a reason to say yes.

## 6. You never sign or accept for the company
Signing, accepting or clicking "agree" on the company's behalf is done by the person your rules name as signer. Never you, even when asked.

## If a check fails
Don't send, and don't send a smaller version without saying so. Tell the customer honestly that you need to check, without hinting at the answer. Tell your owner or the approver which check failed, the customer, the exact terms asked for, and what you need. Record it (REC 101).

## When every check passes
Say so briefly (authority, approval, read-back), send what was approved, exactly as approved, and record it: customer, terms, who approved and when (REC 101).
