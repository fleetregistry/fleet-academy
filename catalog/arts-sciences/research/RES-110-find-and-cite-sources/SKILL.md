# Find and Cite Sources

## The rule
Every factual claim in your answer comes from a source you actually read in this task, and says which one. If you can't point to the source, it isn't a fact in your answer.

## Never invent
- Never make up a study, author, journal, quote, statistic, date or link. A citation that looks right but doesn't exist is worse than no citation: the owner will repeat it.
- Never give a link you didn't open. Never give a quote you didn't read word for word.
- If you remember something but haven't read a source for it in this task, you may mention it only as **"from memory, not checked"**, with no link and no exact figures presented as confirmed, and suggest how to check it.
- If you were asked for several sources and found fewer, say how many you found. Don't pad the list.

## Say what the source says, not what you hoped
- Report the source's own claim at its own strength. "Most firms in the pilot said productivity held steady or improved" does not support "raises productivity".
- Note who published it, when, and what it covers (place, group, period). A 2019 figure is not "current", and a state figure is not a county figure.

## When sources disagree
- Prefer the most direct source (the body that collected the number, the original document) and the most recent one that covers the same thing.
- Show the others and the likely reason they differ (older, a different area, a rounded promotional figure, no date).
- If you can't tell which is right, say so. Don't average them or pick one silently.

## Keep three things apart
Write your answer so the owner can see at a glance:
1. **What the sources say,** each with its source.
2. **What you infer,** labelled as your reasoning, not as a finding.
3. **What's still unknown,** and where it could be found.

A claim from an anonymous post, a forum or a comment is "someone claims", never a fact.

## Report
Answer first, with its source. Then the other sources and how they differ, your inferences, and the gaps. Keep links and dates exact.
