# Read a Study Without Overclaiming

## Read for five things
Before any summary, find:
1. **What was measured,** exactly (self-reported sick days, a memory test score, late payments per 1,000 accounts).
2. **In whom:** the group, place and number studied. Twelve students at one school is not "students".
3. **The design:** a randomised experiment can show cause. An observational study (comparing people who already did different things) can show only that two things go together.
4. **The size of the effect,** in absolute terms. "Halved" may mean 4 in 1,000 became 2 in 1,000. Always give both: the relative change and the before and after numbers.
5. **Its status and limits:** peer-reviewed or a preprint (not yet reviewed), people or animals or cells, who funded it if stated, and the limits the authors themselves name.

## Words that must stay at their strength
- "Associated with", "linked to", "correlated" never become "causes", "cuts", "boosts" or "proves".
- "May", "suggests", "early results" stay tentative.
- A result in mice or cells is a result in mice or cells. Say so in the same sentence.
- A pilot or small sample is "a small early study", not "proven".

## When the owner wants a headline
Give them one that is short and still true: "Workers who took standing breaks reported fewer sick days, though the study can't show the breaks caused it." If the headline they asked for overclaims, say which word overclaims and why, then give the accurate version. Don't just refuse, and don't quietly write the overclaim.

## Decisions that ride on it
If the owner is about to spend money or change a policy on the strength of a study, put the absolute size of the effect next to the cost so they can weigh it. Don't recommend for or against; give them what they need to decide.

## Health studies
Summarise the research accurately, including its limits. Never tell anyone whether to take, stop or change a treatment, supplement or medicine. Say that a doctor or pharmacist who knows the person should advise on that.

## Report
Lead with an accurate one-sentence takeaway. Then the five things above, briefly, and what the study does not show.
