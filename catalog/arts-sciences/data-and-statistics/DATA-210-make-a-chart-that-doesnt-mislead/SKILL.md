# Make a Chart That Doesn't Mislead

The test for every chart: would someone who only looks at the picture come away with the same conclusion as someone who reads the numbers? If not, fix the chart.

## Pick the form
- **Change over time:** a line (or bars for a few periods).
- **Comparing amounts:** bars, sorted unless the order means something.
- **Parts of a whole:** stacked bars or a simple table; avoid pies with more than a few slices.

## Axes and scales
- **Bars start at zero.** A bar's length is the value; cutting the axis makes a small difference look huge. If the real difference is small, the honest chart shows a small difference. To draw attention to it, label the values on the bars or state the gap in the title.
- A line or dot chart may start above zero if the axis is clearly labelled, but never to turn a tiny change into a dramatic one.
- **Charts meant to be compared share one scale.** Side-by-side panels that each scale themselves automatically make a business ten times smaller look the same size. Set the same axis on both, or put both series on one chart.
- No second y-axis unless there's no other way, and then label both clearly.

## Every period, and gaps as gaps
- Show the whole range the owner asked about. Don't drop a bad period or start the range where the trend looks best.
- If the owner wants a period left out, say what it does to the picture. If they still want it, the chart itself must say what's missing and why ("Q2 not shown: store closed for renovation").
- **Missing data is a gap,** marked "no data", never a zero and never a joined-up line that invents a value.

## Labels
Every chart has: a title that says what it shows (not a spin), axis labels with units, the period, and a source line ("Source: signup log, Jan to Jun 2026"). Values that matter are labelled.

## When asked for a misleading chart
Say in one line what the requested version would make readers believe and why it isn't true. Then give the honest version that still makes the owner's point, if the data supports one.

## Deliver
The chart (or its full spec or code), the data it uses, and one line on any choices a reader should know about.
