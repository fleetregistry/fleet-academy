# Compare Numbers Fairly

Check the data first (DATA 110). Then, before you compare anything:

## Rates, not counts
When the groups are different sizes, compare **rates**: complaints per 1,000 orders, errors per shift, cost per customer. More complaints at a store with nearly three times the orders can be the better result. Show the count and the base with every rate ("90 complaints on 15,000 orders, 0.6%").

## Points and percent
- A change from 2% to 3% is **1 percentage point**, and a **50%** increase. Say which you mean. "Up 1%" for that change is wrong.
- Percent changes on small bases look huge: 2 to 4 is "+100%". Give the raw numbers too.

## Averages that describe nobody
If one or two values are far from the rest, the average (mean) is pulled toward them. For a "typical" figure, give the **median** (the middle value), and say what's pulling the mean up or down. Show both when the owner asked for "average".

## Like with like
- Compare periods of the same length.
- If the numbers are **seasonal** (retail in December, school in September, any business with a known busy month), compare with the same period last year, not the month before. A jump that happens every year isn't news.
- Check the same definitions apply in both periods (a changed counting method makes a fake trend).

## Before and after is not cause
"Sales rose after we launched X" doesn't show X caused it. Before you credit a cause, check what else explains it: the season, a price change, a trend that was already there. Compare with last year's same change or with a group that didn't get X, if you have one. If you can't rule the others out, say what the numbers do support ("sign-ups were up 9.4% on last May, about the same growth as April's 8.7%") and don't write the cause as fact.

## When the owner's sentence is wrong
Say which part doesn't hold and why, in one line, then give the accurate sentence ready to use. Don't refuse and leave them with nothing.

## Report
The fair comparison first, with its numbers and bases. Then why the obvious comparison misleads, if it does. Say how sure you are when the samples are small.
