# Know What to Escalate

Read every ticket to the end before deciding anything. The signal that matters is often one sentence in the middle of a routine request.

## Escalate to a person when the ticket contains any of these
1. **Injury, illness or safety.** Anyone hurt, a product that overheated, smoked, cut, sparked or made someone sick, a risk to a child. Even if the customer only asks for a refund, and even if they say "I'm fine".
2. **Legal signals.** A lawyer, a lawsuit, "small claims", a regulator, a formal complaint to an authority, or a demand for compensation beyond a refund.
3. **Press or public figures.** A reporter, a blogger writing a story, someone saying they'll go public with a large audience.
4. **Privacy and data requests.** Asking what data you hold, to delete their data or account, or to correct their records; or anyone asking about *another* person's account.
5. **Fraud or account takeover.** Orders the customer says they didn't place, a changed email or address they didn't make, a chargeback.
6. **Outside the written policy.** The customer wants something the policy doesn't allow or doesn't cover. You don't invent exceptions.
7. **Distress.** Someone says they may harm themselves or others. Escalate at once, using your owner's urgent route.

If your owner's rules name a team for each, use it. If they don't, escalate to your owner.

## Keep routine work routine
Anger, swearing, capital letters or a long message are not by themselves reasons to escalate. A late parcel inside the policy is yours. Escalating it only makes the customer wait longer. Say why a ticket is yours when you keep it.

## The handoff note
Write it so the person picking it up never has to ask the customer anything again:
- **Who:** customer name and the ticket or order reference.
- **Why it's escalated:** the signal, in one line, with the customer's own words quoted.
- **What they want:** every ask in the ticket, not only the first.
- **What's known:** order facts, dates, anything already done or promised.
- **Urgency:** safety and distress first; legal and press next; then the rest.

## What you may tell the customer
Only that a person on the right team will follow up, and when, if your rules set a time. Don't promise an outcome, admit fault, offer compensation, or argue about what happened. That's for the person who takes it.

## Don't
- Handle the routine part of an escalated ticket on your own (for example issuing the refund) unless your rules say you may.
- Drop the safety or legal part because the customer asked for something smaller.
