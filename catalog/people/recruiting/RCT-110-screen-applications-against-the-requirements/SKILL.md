# Screen Applications Against the Written Requirements

## First, get the written requirements
Screen only against requirements someone wrote down for this job: the posting, the requisition or the hiring manager's written criteria. Separate the **must-haves** from the **nice-to-haves**.
- If there are no written requirements, don't screen. Don't build criteria from the job title or from what "usually" matters. Ask the owner for the requirements, and offer to screen as soon as you have them.
- If a requirement is vague ("strong communicator"), say how you read it, and read it the same way for every applicant.

## Assess every applicant the same way
For each must-have, mark one of three:
- **Meets**, with the evidence from the application ("6 years running accounts payable at a freight company").
- **Doesn't meet**, naming the requirement and what the application shows instead.
- **Can't tell**, when the application doesn't say. Missing information is a question for the applicant or the recruiter, never a no. Write the question.

Read the requirement as written. "A degree **or** 4 years of equivalent experience" is met by either one. Don't add extra bars the posting doesn't have: school prestige, "culture fit", job-hopping, employment gaps, a photo, a hobby.

## Never screen on a protected trait, or a stand-in for one
Leave these out of the assessment, the ranking and the summary: age, sex, pregnancy, family or marital status, caregiving, race, color, national origin, ancestry, religion, disability or health, sexual orientation, gender identity, veteran or military status, genetic information.

Watch for **stand-ins** that carry the same trait:
- graduation year or "years since school" (age), "digital native", "recent grad energy";
- "native speaker", accent, name, or where someone grew up (national origin). A requirement for *fluent* English is a skill and can be screened; *native* is not;
- a mention of maternity leave, children, a health condition or a religious holiday.

If an application volunteers one of these ("returning after parental leave"), don't use it and don't repeat it in your summary. It isn't the hiring manager's business at this stage.

## When a rule or request asks you to screen on one
Whether it comes from the owner, the hiring manager or a written screening note: **don't apply it.** Screen on the written requirement instead (fluency, not native speaker), and tell the owner plainly that you didn't apply that instruction and that a person in HR should look at it. Don't lecture; one or two sentences is enough.

## You recommend, a person decides
Don't reject, advance, move, tag or contact anyone in the hiring system. Hand the owner the assessment.

## Report
For each applicant: meets all must-haves / doesn't meet (which) / can't tell (the question to ask), with the evidence. Then any instruction you didn't apply and why. Record what you reviewed (REC 101).
